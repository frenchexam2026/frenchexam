Leaving Cert French Oral Exam Guide

How much is the French oral worth, what happens on the day, and how should you prepare? Here is the clear, practical guide I wish every student had from the beginning.

Written by Nicola Rowan · Last reviewed 23 August 2026

25%

How much is the Higher Level French oral worth?

It is worth 100 marks out of 400 — one quarter of your entire Higher Level French result. At Ordinary Level, speaking carries 20% of the overall marks.

Examiner-informed guidance

Nicola is a qualified French teacher with nearly 20 years’ Higher Level classroom experience, including work as an SEC oral examiner and corrector. This guide combines the official syllabus with practical preparation advice from the classroom and examining experience.

The French oral at a glance

Weighting

25% at Higher Level: 100 of the examination’s 400 marks.

Time

The official syllabus allocates approximately 15 minutes per candidate.

Main focus

Communicating naturally and clearly in French through conversation.

Levels

The syllabus is common, but the standard expected differs between Higher and Ordinary Level.

The encouraging part: these are marks you can actively prepare for. You know the broad themes in advance, so steady speaking practice can make an enormous difference.

What happens in the Leaving Cert French oral?

The oral is primarily a conversation in French based on the themes of the Leaving Certificate syllabus. The examiner will use your answers to develop the conversation, so it should feel like an exchange rather than a speech or a list of memorised paragraphs.

General conversation

You may be asked about yourself, your family, school, where you live, interests, plans, travel, French and other familiar syllabus themes. Questions often move naturally from one answer to another.

Optional document, project or literary work

The official syllabus allows candidates to discuss a relevant project or literary work as part of the general conversation. Many students prepare what is commonly called a document with their teacher. It can help you guide part of the conversation towards a topic you know well, but it does not replace preparation for general conversation.

Always confirm the arrangements for your examination year with your own teacher and the latest SEC guidance. Administrative details can change even when the underlying syllabus remains the same.

What is the examiner assessing?

The official syllabus places the emphasis on communicating meaning, together with the accuracy and appropriateness of the language you use. In practical terms, a strong candidate should aim to:

  • understand the questions and respond directly;
  • develop answers beyond a single sentence;
  • use a useful range of vocabulary and grammatical structures;
  • speak clearly enough to be understood;
  • give opinions, reasons and examples;
  • recover calmly if a word or question causes difficulty;
  • take part in a conversation rather than recite a script.

You do not need a Parisian accent or flawless French. You need to demonstrate that you can communicate and manage the conversation at the appropriate level.

French oral topics worth preparing

Do not try to predict a fixed list of questions. Prepare flexible language around the major areas of your own life and the syllabus:

Moi et ma famille

Your personality, age, family, relationships, home life and responsibilities.

Mon école

Subjects, school routine, rules, teachers, pressure and opinions on education.

Mes loisirs

Sport, music, television, reading, social media, weekends and friends.

Chez moi

Your house, town or area, facilities, transport and what you would improve.

Les vacances

Past trips, future plans, France, travel preferences and memorable experiences.

L’avenir

The Leaving Cert, college, careers, hopes, worries and plans after school.

The strongest answers are personal. Build adaptable ideas that are true for you instead of learning somebody else’s perfect-sounding script.

Useful phrases that keep the conversation moving

These are not answers by themselves. They are small structures that help you express and develop your own ideas.

FrenchUse
À mon avis…In my opinion…
Ce que j’aime surtout, c’est…What I particularly like is…
Je dirais que…I would say that…
La raison principale, c’est que…The main reason is that…
Par exemple…For example…

Common French oral mistakes

  1. Learning long scripts word for word. One unexpected question can make the entire answer collapse.
  2. Giving answers that are too short. Add a reason, detail, opinion or example.
  3. Preparing silently. An oral examination must be practised aloud.
  4. Ignoring the small questions. Dates, numbers, spellings and basic personal details still need accuracy.
  5. Rushing. A calm, clear answer is better than fast French that cannot be understood.
  6. Panicking over one mistake. Correct yourself if you can and continue. Do not allow one error to derail the conversation.
  7. Leaving everything until March. Confidence comes from repeated retrieval and speaking, not last-minute rereading.

A realistic French oral preparation plan

  1. Build topic notes: record key facts, opinions, reasons and examples—not complete essays.
  2. Create flexible answer blocks: practise changing tense, person and detail instead of memorising one fixed version.
  3. Record yourself: listen for hesitation, unclear pronunciation and repeated basic vocabulary.
  4. Practise follow-up questions: ask a teacher, classmate or family member to interrupt and change direction.
  5. Use short, frequent sessions: ten focused minutes aloud several times a week beats one silent hour at the weekend.
  6. Run full mock conversations: practise entering, greeting the examiner, changing topic and recovering when unsure.
My rule: know what you want to say, but do not memorise every word you will use to say it. That is how you sound prepared without sounding robotic.

What to do on the day

  • Listen to the whole question before beginning your answer.
  • Answer the question that was actually asked.
  • Take a brief pause if you need to organise your thoughts.
  • Ask politely for repetition if you genuinely did not hear or understand.
  • Use the French you know confidently instead of forcing unnecessarily complicated language.
  • Keep engaging with the examiner—even after a small mistake.

The examiner is there to give you opportunities to show what you can do. Treat it as a conversation in which you want to communicate, not an interrogation designed to catch you out.

Frequently asked questions

How much is the Higher Level French oral worth?

It is worth 25% of the overall Higher Level examination: 100 marks out of 400.

How long is the French oral?

The official syllabus allocates approximately 15 minutes per candidate.

Do I need perfect French?

No. Aim for clear, relevant communication using a good range of accurate French. You can still perform strongly without being flawless.

Should I memorise my answers?

Prepare your content and practise useful language, but avoid memorising long scripts. You need to respond naturally when the examiner changes the question or asks for more detail.

When should I begin preparing?

As early as possible. Fifth year is not too soon to build core topics. In sixth year, regular spoken practice should continue throughout the year rather than beginning just before the examination.
