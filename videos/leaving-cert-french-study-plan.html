Higher Level Leaving Certificate French

A Realistic Leaving Cert French Study Plan

A weekly plan for balancing oral, listening, reading, writing, vocabulary and grammar—without trying to “do French” in one enormous session.

Written by Nicola Rowan · Last reviewed 23 August 2026

The best French study plan is one you can repeat

Three to five focused sessions spread across the week will usually achieve more than a single exhausting revision block. French is a skill subject: improvement comes from regular retrieval, use and correction.

A teacher-built plan

Nicola is a qualified French teacher with nearly 20 years’ Higher Level classroom experience, including work as an SEC oral examiner and corrector. The plan below is designed for real school weeks—not imaginary students with unlimited time.

Start with a five-minute skills audit

Before making a timetable, rate each area honestly: oral, listening, reading comprehension, written production, grammar and vocabulary. Mark each as secure, developing or urgent.

Your weakest area deserves extra attention, but do not abandon the others. A student who spends months writing essays while ignoring oral and aural practice is leaving 45% of the Higher Level examination underprepared.

Do not confuse familiarity with ability. Looking over notes can feel comfortable. The test is whether you can retrieve and use the French without the answer in front of you.

A balanced weekly French study plan

Adjust the days around homework, sport and other subjects. The mix matters more than the exact timetable.

Session 1
15–20 minOral retrieval: answer one topic aloud without reading, record it, then improve weak sections.
Session 2
20–25 minListening: complete a short extract, check it, use the transcript and replay the difficult parts.
Session 3
30–40 minWriting: plan and complete one timed paragraph or exam task, then check recurring errors.
Session 4
20–30 minReading: work through part of a comprehension and identify useful vocabulary in context.
Session 5
15–20 minGrammar and vocabulary: revise one targeted problem and retrieve the week’s new language.

You can combine shorter sessions on busy weeks. Avoid dropping speaking and listening merely because written work feels easier to organise.

What to do in each skill area

Oral

Build personal topic notes, practise follow-up questions and speak aloud several times each week.

Listening

Answer, check, analyse and replay. Background audio alone is not a complete strategy.

Reading

Use past questions, justify answers from the text and record vocabulary in useful phrases.

Writing

Practise planning, relevance and accuracy across opinion, narrative, diary and practical formats.

Grammar

Target errors that actually occur in your work instead of endlessly rereading every rule.

Vocabulary

Learn language in short phrases and retrieve it repeatedly rather than copying isolated lists.

How your priorities should change through the year

September to December: build the system

Establish weekly habits, strengthen core grammar and vocabulary, create oral topic material and begin regular listening. Practise individual writing skills before expecting perfect timed answers.

January to the oral: increase retrieval

Complete mock conversations, strengthen weak oral topics and practise recovering from unexpected questions. Continue written and listening work—the oral should receive extra focus, not consume the entire subject.

After the oral: rebalance quickly

Move the time previously used for speaking into listening, comprehensions and written production. Use timed tasks and past papers to expose remaining gaps.

Final weeks: practise performance

Revise high-value language, complete selected timed sections and correct familiar errors. Do not attempt to learn a new encyclopaedia of essays immediately before the exam.

A sensible plan for fifth year

Fifth year is the time to build foundations without panic. Concentrate on accurate core tenses, useful topic vocabulary, confident personal conversation and regular contact with spoken French.

Begin an oral folder and an error log now. Write shorter pieces well before chasing full exam length. By the end of fifth year, aim to understand the principal exam formats and have study habits that can scale up in sixth year.

If you feel behind

  1. Stop trying to cover everything simultaneously. Choose one urgent weakness and one maintenance skill each week.
  2. Prioritise high-frequency foundations: present, past and future time; opinions; reasons; descriptions; questions; negatives and agreements.
  3. Use exam tasks diagnostically. Every mistake tells you what to revise next.
  4. Prepare adaptable language. Do not spend scarce time memorising several complete essays.
  5. Track completed work. A short written record makes progress visible and reduces panic.
You do not need to feel “caught up” before practising exam skills. Practise them now at your current level and improve through the process.

Your Sunday five-minute review

  • Did I speak French aloud this week?
  • Did I complete focused listening and check why I missed answers?
  • Did I produce French from memory rather than only reread notes?
  • Did I correct or rewrite a piece of work?
  • What is the single most useful focus for next week?

Frequently asked questions

How many hours should I study French each week?

There is no perfect number. Begin with three to five focused sessions outside class and adjust for your level, homework and proximity to examinations. Consistency and active practice matter more than merely counting hours.

Should I study French every day?

Short daily contact can be excellent, especially for speaking and vocabulary, but your plan must be sustainable. Several focused sessions every week can still produce strong progress.

Which part should I prioritise?

Give additional time to your weakest high-value area while maintaining all four skills. Use actual completed work—not guesswork—to identify the weakness.

Is it too late to improve?

No. Narrow the plan, practise actively and correct recurring errors. Focused work from your current level is more valuable than panicked attempts to restart the entire course.
