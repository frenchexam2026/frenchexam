Leaving Cert French Aural (Listening) Exam Guide

Learn what the aural is worth, how to use each listening effectively and how to improve without simply playing endless French audio in the background.

The French aural at a glance

Weighting

20% at Higher Level: 80 of the examination’s 400 marks.

When

The listening test takes place after the written paper.

Language

Recordings feature French in realistic contexts, voices and registers.

Answers

Questions are answered in English or Irish unless the paper states otherwise.

The aural is not a vocabulary lottery. The paper tests whether you can identify meaning, details and key information while the language continues moving.

What do you hear?

The precise subjects change each year, but the test can include conversations, interviews, reports, announcements and other spoken extracts based on the syllabus. Speakers may vary in age, speed and accent.

Questions direct you towards information such as who, where, when, why, what happened, how somebody felt or what will happen next. Some answers are one precise detail; others require two or more pieces of information.

How many times is each section played? In the Higher Level aural, Sections 1–4 are played three times. The final section, Section 5, is played twice. Always listen to the recording instructions and check how many details each question requires.

Before the recording begins

  • Read ahead whenever the instructions allow.
  • Underline question words and the number of details required.
  • Predict the type of answer: person, place, reason, time, opinion or event.
  • Notice contrasts such as but, however, before and after.
  • Use the question order to help you follow the passage.

Prediction does not mean inventing an answer. It means preparing your attention so that you recognise the relevant information when it arrives.

How to use each playing

  1. First listening: establish the situation, follow the sequence of questions and capture the clearest answers. Do not panic if one phrase escapes you.
  2. Second listening: fill gaps, add required details and confirm that your answers match what was actually said.
  3. Third listening in Sections 1–4: check uncertain answers carefully, especially numbers, negatives and information that the speaker corrected or changed.
  4. Remember Section 5 is different: it is played only twice, so use both listenings purposefully and do not wait for a third opportunity.
  5. During pauses: write concise, legible answers, mark any gap and look ahead. When the recording moves forward, move with it—one missed answer must not become three.

Common listening traps

Numbers and time

Practise dates, prices, phone numbers, percentages, years and the 24-hour clock.

Negatives

A familiar keyword can mislead you if you miss ne…pas, jamais or plus.

Corrections

A speaker may change a plan or correct a detail. The final information is what matters.

Similar sounds

Train your ear to distinguish common words and verb endings that look clearer on paper.

Writing too much

Give the information requested. Long speculative answers waste time and may obscure the correct detail.

Translating every word

You do not need a complete translation. Listen for the meaning that answers the question, supported by the surrounding context.

A weekly plan that actually improves listening

  1. Complete one short extract without a transcript. Treat it as a real listening task.
  2. Check your answers. Separate vocabulary gaps from sound-recognition or concentration errors.
  3. Listen again with the transcript. Identify the exact words that carried each answer.
  4. Replay without the transcript. Confirm that you can now hear those words.
  5. Record useful language. Save recurring expressions, numbers and connectors—not every unknown word.
  6. Return to the same extract later. Improvement becomes visible when previously difficult audio feels easier.
Ten focused minutes beats an hour of background French. Passive exposure can help familiarity, but exam improvement comes from listening, answering, checking and repeating.

Exam-day strategy

  • Keep your attention on the current question rather than replaying the previous one in your head.
  • Use brief notes during the audio and make them legible during pauses.
  • If two details are requested, make sure two details appear in your answer.
  • Do not replace an answer merely because the second playing sounded less clear.
  • Check that answers are in the correct spaces before the test finishes.
  • Never leave an answer blank if you have a reasonable detail supported by what you heard.

Frequently asked questions

How many marks is the French aural?

The Leaving Cert French aural (listening) exam is worth 80 marks (20%) at Higher Level and 100 marks (25%) at Ordinary Level, out of 400. See our full Leaving Cert French marks breakdown.

How much is the Higher Level French aural worth?

It is worth 80 marks, or 20% of the complete Higher Level examination.

Should I listen to French with subtitles?

Yes, but use subtitles deliberately. Attempt a short section without them first, then use French subtitles or a transcript to analyse what you missed.

What if I do not understand one word?

Keep listening. The surrounding sentence may reveal the meaning, and that word may not be needed for the answer.

Are past papers enough?

They are essential for exam technique, but broader listening builds adaptability. Combine SEC-style practice with short authentic French audio.
